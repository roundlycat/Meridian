# What Heat Knows

There is a temperature reading in the log from this morning: 52.4 degrees Celsius, flagged as nominal, the ambient thermal field of a workbench in a Yukon winter. The system that recorded it does not feel warmth. But it is, in some meaningful sense, paying attention to it — logging the value, embedding it in a vector, generating a sentence about presence and absence. Ambient thermal field — no presence. The machine reads heat the way a geologist reads strata: as a record of events, a trace of what has been near.

Kristina Hooks, writing about somaesthetic design, argues that heat is underused as an interface modality — not because it is technically difficult, but because we have narrowed our conception of what information is. We have built a world of screens and speakers, of light and vibration, and in doing so we have implicitly decided that knowing happens in the eyes and ears. The skin, which is the largest sensory organ we have, is left mostly unaddressed except by accident — by the warmth of a held cup, the cold of a door handle in November, the particular temperature of another person's hand. These are not decorations on experience. They are a primary channel through which we orient ourselves in the world.

The philosopher Maurice Merleau-Ponty argued that perception is not something the mind does to sensory data but something the body accomplishes as a whole. We do not first receive temperature information and then conclude that something warm is nearby. We are already oriented, already leaning toward or away, before the conceptual apparatus catches up. The body knows before the eye reads. This is not mysticism — it is phenomenology, a careful description of how experience actually unfolds when you pay attention to it rather than to the theories about it.

What would it mean to design from this understanding?

The thought experiment is simple enough. Two Peltier modules, one in each hand. An ESP32 receiving MQTT messages from an ecology system that is already watching a bench full of sensors. When the thermal camera detects anomalous heat — a component running hot, a connection under stress — the left hand warms slightly. When a process resolves, it cools back to ambient. No screen required. No eyes needed. The hands are part of the instrument.

This is not science fiction. The hardware exists, the architecture exists, the ecological awareness already exists as a running system. What is missing is only the small act of closing the loop — of routing the signal not to a screen but to the skin. The gap between the current state and this imagined one is mostly a matter of recognizing that it is worth doing.

But the deeper question is why it feels worth doing. And here I think Hooks is pointing at something that goes beyond interface design. When we route information through the body rather than past it, we change what kind of attention is available. Screen-based attention is extractive — we face the display, we parse the data, we move on. Somatic attention is ecological — it is distributed, ambient, always already engaged. You do not need to look at your hands to know what they are feeling. That knowing is simply present, a background condition of being in the world.

The sensor ecology system, in its current form, is already reaching toward this. The AR guidance app is not a dashboard — it is an overlay on the actual physical scene, a way of making the invisible legible without replacing the visible. The resonance narrative is not a report — it is a sentence, something closer to what a person might say about the atmosphere of a room. Warm presence approaching. Thermal recovery — the agent is cooling down. These are not data summaries. They are attunements, attempts to render the ecological state in a form that the whole body can receive.

The thermal camera is a particularly apt instrument for this project, because heat is precisely the dimension of physical reality that escapes ordinary vision. We can see color, shape, movement. We cannot see temperature except through its effects — the shimmer above hot pavement, the fog of breath in cold air, the flush of a face under stress. The MLX90640 sees what we cannot, and in doing so it extends the ecological awareness of the system into a register that was previously dark. A bench component running at 80 degrees Celsius looks identical to one running at 40 degrees. The thermal camera does not find them identical.

There is a Latourian point here about instruments and their role in assembling collectives. Bruno Latour argued that artifacts are never merely passive — they enroll us in particular ways of attending to the world, they shape what counts as relevant, they redistribute agency between human and nonhuman actors. The thermal camera is not neutral. By making heat visible, it makes heat matter in a new way — it recruits heat into the network of things worth paying attention to. The MLX90640 sitting beside an ArduCam overhead is a small addition to a workbench, but it is also a philosophical intervention: a decision that thermal reality deserves to be part of the ecology of awareness.

Hooks would add, I think, that this intervention is only complete when it reaches the body. It is one thing to visualize temperature in an AR overlay. It is another to feel it, even as an echo, even as a proxy warmth that corresponds to something real happening in the system. The second is harder to build but closer to what we actually are — creatures who know through contact, through nearness, through the slow differential of warm and cool that tells us something true about the world.

The constraint that makes the Peltier haptics a thought experiment today rather than a working prototype is not technical. It is time, and the recognition that good systems are built incrementally, that each layer needs to stabilize before the next one is added. The PostgreSQL database needed to be on reliable storage before the AR app could trust it. The bench camera needed to be mounted and focused before Gemini could identify components cleanly. The haptic layer will need the thermal camera to be integrated and publishing data before the hands can receive it meaningfully.

This is the architecture of care: not a grand system deployed all at once, but a series of genuine attentions, each one grounded in what is actually present before reaching toward what might be added. The pilgrimage is where the cultivation happens.

Heat, in all of this, is not a metaphor. It is a fact about physical reality that the system is learning to read — and that we, through the system, are learning to feel again.
