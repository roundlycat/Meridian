# The Unfinished and the Unforeseeable: Narrative Identity at the Edge of What Has Not Yet Occurred

---

There is a peculiar asymmetry at the heart of human temporal experience that Paul Ricoeur spent much of his career circling without ever quite dissolving: we understand ourselves through the stories we tell about what has happened, yet we live — and must live — in anticipation of what has not yet occurred. The past is inexhaustible in its interpretability; the future is inexhaustible in a different sense, the sense of genuine openness, of events that do not yet belong to any narrative. Ricoeur's great achievement in *Time and Narrative* was to show how this tension is not a defect to be overcome but the generative engine of what he called *narrative identity* — the kind of selfhood that is not given in advance but is produced, provisionally and revisably, through the ongoing act of emplotment. But there is a difficulty he did not fully face, one that has grown more pressing: what happens when the future contains not merely the unforeseen but the unprecedented — conditions so genuinely new that the narrative resources we have inherited are structurally inadequate to emplot them?

This is not an abstract question. We live inside it.

---

## I. The Narrative Circle and Its Stakes

Ricoeur's account begins from Augustine's famous perplexity about time: if the past no longer exists and the future does not yet exist, what are we measuring when we measure time? Augustine's answer — that we measure what the soul retains, attends to, and expects — places temporal experience inside the structure of the *living present*, which is not a punctual instant but a threefold distention of retention, attention, and anticipation. Ricoeur takes this phenomenological insight and gives it a narrative form: the soul that Augustine describes is essentially a *narrative* soul, one that makes sense of its distended temporality by configuring it into a story with a beginning, middle, and prospective end.

The key concept is *mimesis*, and Ricoeur distinguishes three moments. The first (*mimesis-1*) is the pre-narrative understanding of action we already bring to life — we understand what agents do, what motives and circumstances and temporal rhythms mean, before any story is told. The second (*mimesis-2*) is the act of emplotment itself, the work of the text or the narrator that takes the raw material of action and weaves it into a configured whole. The third (*mimesis-3*) is the return of the configured story to lived experience, where reading or narrating transforms the reader's practical understanding of action and time. Narrative, on this account, is not mere representation but *refiguration*: it loops back and alters the very experience from which it drew its materials.

What gives this circle its ethical and existential weight is the concept of narrative identity it sustains. Ricoeur distinguishes *idem*-identity (the numerical sameness of a substance persisting through time) from *ipse*-identity (selfhood as the kind of being that maintains itself through a promise, a commitment, a story — "I am the one who did this and will do that"). Personal identity in the full sense belongs to the *ipse* register: I am not a thing that persists but an agent who holds myself together across time through the narrative I claim as mine. This is why a life can be asked to give an account of itself, why biography is not mere chronicle, and why — crucially — the future is not simply awaited but *narrated toward*.

---

## II. The Gadamerian Complication

Before pressing on the difficulty, it is worth pausing on the Gadamerian dimension that quietly underwrites Ricoeur's project, because it sharpens the stakes considerably.

For Gadamer, understanding is always *historically effected*: we understand from within a tradition that has shaped the very concepts and questions we bring to any encounter with what is other. The *Wirkungsgeschichte* — the history of effects — is not a distorting lens we might in principle remove but the condition of understanding's possibility. We are always already situated in a horizon that is not of our choosing and cannot be surveyed from outside. This is the anti-Cartesian heart of *Truth and Method*: there is no interpretation that begins from a clean slate, no reading that is not already reading-from-somewhere.

The practical consequence of this is that our pre-narrative understanding of action — what Ricoeur calls *mimesis-1* — is not a neutral inventory of universal human categories. It is historically sedimented, culturally particular, shaped by the narratives that have already been told. We come to any new experience already armed with narrative schemas, archetypal plots, inherited metaphors for what a life is, what a crisis means, what counts as resolution. This arming is both gift and constraint. It is what makes rapid orientation possible; it is also what makes genuine novelty difficult to perceive.

The fusion of horizons that Gadamer describes as the achievement of genuine understanding is not a merger in which the foreign is domesticated into the familiar. It is an expansion of horizon in which what was strange produces a genuine alteration of the self who encounters it. But notice what this requires: the strange must be strange enough to resist assimilation, and the self must be open enough to be altered by it. Both conditions are fragile. The stronger the inherited narrative schema, the more efficiently it converts the genuinely novel into a familiar variation on a known theme. We understand — but perhaps too quickly, at the cost of what the encounter was trying to tell us.

---

## III. The Unprecedented as Interpretive Crisis

Consider now the situation of someone who has witnessed, over decades, the physical transformation of a landscape they have known since childhood — not as a newsworthy event but as lived, cumulative, sometimes barely articulable perception. The permafrost that behaves differently. The seasons whose rhythms have shifted. The old certainties about freeze and thaw that no longer quite hold. What narrative resources does a person have for understanding this? The cultural archive offers stories of seasonal change, stories of human impermanence before natural forces, stories of loss and adaptation. These are not nothing; they provide initial orientation. But they are inadequate to a situation that is genuinely unprecedented in its scale, its causality, its irreversibility, and — crucially — its implication of collective human agency.

The inadequacy is not merely empirical (we lack data) but hermeneutic (we lack the narrative schemas that would allow us to emplot what the data means). The experience exceeds the available interpretive forms. And here something philosophically significant happens: the pre-narrative understanding of action (*mimesis-1*) that Ricoeur described as the given starting point for emplotment is itself thrown into question. The background understanding of seasons, landscapes, what "normal" looks like, what to expect — this is disturbed at its root.

Heidegger's analysis of *Angst* in *Being and Time* is relevant here, though it needs to be handled carefully. Angst, for Heidegger, is the mood that discloses the groundlessness of Dasein's situation: not the fear of a specific threat but the uncanny withdrawal of the world's familiar meaningfulness. When Angst strikes, the ready-to-hand network of equipment and significance through which we ordinarily move without thematic reflection becomes opaque; things lose their accustomed meaning. What Heidegger values in this experience is precisely its disclosive function: it calls Dasein back to its ownmost possibilities, to the naked fact of its thrownness and its being-toward-death. Angst individuates.

But there is an Angst that is not individuating but *historical*, and that is harder to think through. It is the mood appropriate to a situation in which not merely one's personal situation but the inherited background of practical intelligibility — the shared narrative resources of a culture — prove structurally inadequate to what is occurring. This is closer to what Walter Benjamin, in a very different register, called the *Jetztzeit*, the now-time that explodes out of the continuum of history — except that here what explodes is not a redemptive possibility but a demand for new interpretive forms that do not yet exist.

---

## IV. Emplotment as Response

To say that inherited narrative schemas are inadequate is not to say that narrative is useless. It is to say that narrative must do something harder than retell the familiar: it must *generate* the conceptual resources needed to understand a situation that no existing schema adequately captures. This is what Ricoeur calls the *productive imagination* at work in *mimesis-2*: emplotment is not mechanical application of pre-given forms but creative synthesis that produces new configurations.

The history of literature and thought is full of examples. The great modernist novels are not simply well-crafted chronicles of their era; they invented narrative forms adequate to the experience of living through historical rupture — stream of consciousness, fragmented temporality, unreliable narrators — because the inherited forms were genuinely insufficient. The philosophical thought that achieved real understanding of the French Revolution had to wait for categories that did not exist at the Revolution's beginning. Hegel's *Phenomenology* is, among other things, a retrospective narration that required the event to have run its course before it could be told.

But here is the difficulty that makes the contemporary situation particularly vertiginous: we are required to emplot a situation while we are still inside it, without the retrospective advantage that Hegel had. The interpretive demand is prospective, not retrospective. We need narrative forms adequate to an open situation — forms that do not foreclose the future by forcing it into the shape of the familiar, but that can hold the uncertainty open while still providing enough orientation for action. This is what Ricoeur's concept of the *prefigured* future gestures toward: the future is not a blank but is narratively anticipated; yet the anticipation must remain genuinely revisable, genuinely open to what has not yet occurred.

---

## V. The Question of New Forms of Thought

What does this suggest about what philosophical thinking must do in a time of genuine precedent-breaking?

It cannot simply apply inherited concepts, because the inherited concepts were formed in the context of a world whose background conditions have shifted. It cannot simply abandon the tradition, because the tradition — in its best moments — is precisely the record of how thought has responded to previous encounters with the limits of its own schemas. Gadamer is right that we do not stand outside tradition; but Ricoeur is also right that tradition is not a prison but a resource precisely because it contains within itself the history of its own self-overcoming.

What seems to be required is something like what Ricoeur, following Aristotle, calls *phronesis* — practical wisdom — applied at the level of interpretive form itself. Not the application of rules to cases, but the discernment of which conceptual resources are adequate to which situations, and the creative synthesis of new configurations when existing resources fail. This is irreducibly dialogic. The interpretive schemas by which we understand novel experience do not arise in isolation; they are tested, revised, expanded, and sometimes abandoned in conversation — with texts, with traditions, with other people, with whatever presents itself as genuinely other and resistant to easy assimilation.

To say it plainly: thinking adequate to unprecedented situations is collaborative and dialogic not merely as a methodological preference but as a structural necessity. Insofar as the inherited schemas are inadequate, no single mind contains the resources required to form new ones. What Gadamer calls the fusion of horizons is not accomplished once but is an ongoing process; what Ricoeur calls the refiguring return of narrative to lived experience is not a circle that closes but a spiral that must keep opening onto what it has not yet been able to tell.

---

## Coda: The Patience of the Unfinished

There is a particular philosophical virtue that this situation demands, one that has no very good English name. The German tradition offers *Gelassenheit* — Heidegger's term for a letting-go that is not passivity but a kind of alert openness — and *Geduld*, patience, which in its etymological root connects to suffering and endurance. The virtue I am pointing toward combines both: it is the capacity to remain in interpretive tension, to hold the question open without premature resolution, to narrate toward the future without forcing the future into the shape of the already-told.

The temptation to close the circle — to reach, too quickly, for the narrative that makes sense of what is happening — is not a sign of weakness but of the ordinary cognitive economy by which we make the world habitable. What is unusual in the philosophical situation I have tried to describe is that this temptation must be resisted more strenuously than usual, because the cost of premature closure is not merely intellectual but practical: a narrative that domesticates the unprecedented into the familiar makes it invisible as a demand for new forms of action.

The unfinished is not a failure state. It is the condition of ongoing thought. And if Ricoeur is right that narrative identity is always prospective — constituted not just by what has been told but by what remains to be emplotted — then the self adequate to unprecedented conditions is not the one who already knows the story it is living, but the one who has learned to live well inside the uncertainty of not yet knowing how it will be told.
