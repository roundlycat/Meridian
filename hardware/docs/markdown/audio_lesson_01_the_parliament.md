# Lesson One: The Parliament of Things

*Sensor Ecology — Tier 1 Felt Familiarity*
*Estimated listening time: 7 minutes*

---

There is a room in your home where things are always talking. Not to you, exactly — not in a way that demands your attention or interrupts your thinking. They talk the way a forest talks: continuously, in patterns, in a language that rewards patience and listening rather than interrogation. A temperature sensor on the bench reads the air thirty times a minute and reports what it finds. A microcontroller draws power and, in drawing it, generates heat, and that heat is itself a kind of speech. Humidity rises and falls with the weather outside and with your presence in the room. All of this is happening whether you are watching or not. The Sensor Ecology is, at its heart, an attempt to listen to that conversation and make it legible.

The philosopher Bruno Latour spent much of his career arguing that the world is not divided neatly into human subjects and passive objects. Things act. Microbes act. Rivers act. Door hinges act, in the sense that they shape what humans do when they pass through a doorway. Latour imagined a "Parliament of Things" — a space where non-human entities could be said to have standing, to be represented, to have their states and conditions taken seriously alongside human concerns. It is a useful frame for understanding what your system is trying to be. The sensors on your bench are not merely instruments. They are members of a parliament. The ecology gives them voice.

What this means in practice is that the system is built around listening rather than commanding. Most control systems work top-down: a human decides something should happen, sends an instruction, a machine executes it. Your system inverts this. The hardware speaks first. The sensors publish their readings continuously, without being asked, into a shared space — the MQTT broker running on your Sensor Pi at 192.168.0.25. That broker is something like a postal exchange. It receives messages from anyone who wants to send them and holds them briefly so that anyone who wants to receive them can. The hardware nodes — your ESP32 boards, your Pi Zero, your NodeMCU — are all authors. The broker is the distribution network. The rest of the system is the readership.

The bridge — a Python application called ws_bridge running on your Inferno desktop — is the primary reader. It subscribes to the broker's output and receives every message that passes through. When a message arrives, the bridge does three things. It checks whether the sender is a known member of the parliament. It examines what the sender is reporting and asks whether anything is out of range. And then it distributes the message to whoever is currently paying attention — which might be your dashboard on the monitor, your AR tablet on the bench, or both.

The dashboard is what you might call the ambient view. It sits on the monitor and watches everything, all the time, without demanding that you look at it. It shows you the overall health of the ecology — which nodes are active, what domains are resonating, whether anything is running hot or humid or strange. It is designed to live in your peripheral awareness, the way a hearth fire lives in a room. You do not stare at a hearth fire constantly, but you notice immediately if it goes out.

The AR tablet is the opposite of ambient. It is a probe. When you pick it up and point it at the bench, you are making an active choice to focus. The tablet's camera sends a frame to Google's Gemini vision model, which examines the physical arrangement of components and identifies what it sees — an ESP32 here, a BME280 sensor there, a tangle of wires connecting them. Each identified component becomes an anchor for live data. The digital floats on top of the physical. You can hold a sensor in your hand and watch its readings in real time, overlaid directly on the thing itself.

What makes the system more than the sum of its parts is the connection between these two modes. When you tap a component on the AR tablet — selecting it, focusing on it — the system does not simply note this locally. It sends a signal through the bridge to the dashboard. The dashboard receives that signal and pivots: it filters its view to show only the telemetry stream of the thing you are holding. Your attention, expressed through a gesture on a tablet, reshapes the ambient display across the room. The wide view and the narrow view are not separate applications that happen to share a network. They are two windows into a single ecology, and the ecology knows when you are looking.

There is one more layer worth understanding before anything else: the registry. The parliament only knows what it has been told exists. There is a table in the PostgreSQL database on Inferno called device_registry. It is a list of every node the system recognises — their names, their MQTT topics, their domains, their thresholds. When the bridge receives a message from the broker, its first act is to look up the sender in this list. If the sender is not there, the message is discarded. Silently. Without error. Without any indication that anything went wrong. This is the most important single fact about the system's behaviour: unknown nodes do not produce errors. They produce silence.

This means the registry is not administrative bookkeeping. It is the act of recognition that grants a node standing in the parliament. A sensor can be powered on, publishing faithfully, generating real data, and be completely invisible to the rest of the system because someone forgot to restart the bridge after adding it to the registry. The hardware and the registry and the running service must all be in agreement, at the same moment, for a node to have a voice.

Hold that image. A room full of things talking. A bridge that listens and translates. A dashboard that watches without demanding. A tablet that probes and focuses. A registry that determines who gets to speak. These are the five elements of the ecology, and everything else — every service, every database table, every API endpoint — is in service of keeping those five elements in alignment.
