# On Standing Where You Can See: Embodiment, the Click, and the Search for Realistic Hope

---

We began this morning with a horse.

Kristina Höök opens her work on somatic design with the image of riding, and it is worth staying with that choice longer than might first seem necessary, because it is doing a great deal of philosophical work quietly. When you ride well, something strange happens that doesn't happen with any ordinary tool. The horse doesn't become transparent in the way a hammer does when you are hammering — Heidegger's famous analysis of ready-to-hand equipment describes how a well-functioning tool disappears from conscious attention into your purpose, becoming an invisible extension of your will. But the horse never quite does this, and the reason it doesn't is philosophically important rather than merely biological. The horse resists disappearance because it is another center of experience. It has a bad day. It carries the memory of previous riders in the tension of its responses. It reads you — your breath, the shift of your weight, the tightening of your thighs — and responds before you have consciously decided anything. And crucially, it knows when you have stopped relating to it and started merely using it, and when that happens the quality of the whole interaction degrades.

What Höök has found in this image is an example where the somatic relationship and the ethical relationship are the same relationship. To ride well is to remain in what Martin Buber would call I-Thou mode even while pursuing a directed, practical goal. The moment you collapse into I-It — into pure instrumentalization — the horse tells you immediately through the quality of movement, through resistance, through something going wrong that has no name except the absence of what was there before. Skilled riders know this phenomenologically before they have words for it. The relationship requires a kind of sustained attention that is neither contemplative nor merely utilitarian but something in between, a mode of engaged mutual recognition that most of our theories of tool use have no category for.

This is why Höök's choice of the horse is not sentimental. She is not appealing to nature or to some pre-technological authenticity. She is identifying a structure — mutual somatic constitution, meaning made in the space between two embodied beings, neither of whom is simply sending signals to the other — and asking what it would mean to design for that structure rather than designing around it or against it.

---

The soma, in Höök's framework drawing on Richard Shusterman's somaesthetics, is not the body as object, not the body as instrument, but the body as the site where meaning is made before it is represented. This is Merleau-Ponty's great contribution, refined and extended: that perception is always already motoric, that we grasp the world through schemas of possible action before we ever form a mental representation of it. When you walk into a room, you have already assessed ceiling height, floor surface, the affordances of furniture, the distances between things, not as explicit calculations but as a kind of postural intelligence that precedes and enables conscious thought. The body knows before the mind knows, and what the body knows is not simply information but meaning — the felt sense of whether a space is welcoming or threatening, navigable or complex, familiar or strange.

Shusterman's somaesthetics rehabilitates somatic experience as a legitimate domain of cultivation and knowledge. You can get better at attending to somatic experience, more skilled at reading it and acting from it, in the way you can get better at any practice. This is not mysticism; it is recognizing that a dimension of human knowing that has been systematically devalued by a philosophical tradition that privileges disembodied rationality is actually doing foundational epistemic work all the time, whether we acknowledge it or not.

Höök inherits this and asks the design question: what would it mean to design not for the body's inputs, not around the body's limitations, not even ergonomically for the body's affordances, but with the body as a generative site of meaning-making that has its own logic — temporal, kinetic, affective — which digital systems typically destroy when they don't account for it?

This is a harder question than it first appears, and it's worth dwelling on why. The obvious response to "we've neglected the body in computing" is to add haptic feedback, to make interfaces more tangible, to restore physical manipulation to digital objects. And this is not nothing — the work coming out of MIT's Tangible Media Group, Hiroshi Ishii's Tangible Bits project, represented a genuine insight that the GUI's dematerialization of interaction had severed computing from the proprioceptive and kinesthetic channels through which we understand and remember physical objects. A physical token you can grasp, orient, hand across a table carries epistemic content that a clicked icon cannot. The tangible approach tries to restore that.

But Höök's deeper point runs beneath this. Tangible computing, for all its sophistication, still tends to treat the body as a richer input mechanism. The conceptual structure is still: mind forms intention, body enacts it through enriched physical affordances, system responds. What she wants is a reconception where the felt quality of the interaction is the meaning, not a delivery system for meaning — where the soma and the system are in genuine mutual constitution rather than a cleaner version of the traditional mind-tool relationship. The horse model again, not the hammer model, even when the hammer has a pleasant grip.

---

There is a point, however, where the somatic perspective can slide into a mistake, and it is worth naming it. There is a temptation to treat somatic knowing as more authentic than computational or abstract knowing, as if bringing the body back is always a corrective move, as if the digital is always an impoverishment. This would be a mistake because it misunderstands what the soma actually is.

Merleau-Ponty is clear that the body schema extends into the tools we habitually use. The experienced driver doesn't consciously calculate the width of the car when navigating a narrow lane; the car's edges have become part of the proprioceptive field, extensions of the felt body. The same is true for any skilled practitioner with their tools. The philosopher who has spent decades with philosophical argument develops a somatic sense of conceptual structure — a felt discomfort when an argument has a hidden gap, a physical analogue to the aesthetic recognition that something is too heavy on one side, an almost postural sense of when a position is untenable. These are somatic events even when they occur entirely at a screen.

So the question for interaction design isn't how to restore the body but rather what kinds of somatic extension a system cultivates, and whether those extensions are worth cultivating. Some systems train us into habits of attention and responsiveness that enrich our capacity for meaning-making. Others narrow us, make us less responsive to subtlety, reduce our tolerance for ambiguity, optimize our engagement in ways that serve the system's purposes rather than our flourishing. The soma doesn't stop at the epidermis. The question is always: what kind of body does this practice make?

---

Let us stay for a while with the click.

You described finding something resonant in the somatic dimensions of understanding, and in particular in that moment — known to anyone who has ever genuinely understood something new — when a whole perspective shifts almost instantaneously, and then cascades. It is worth thinking carefully about what actually happens in that moment, because the usual ways of describing it are almost certainly wrong about its most interesting features.

The cognitive science literature tends to treat insight as a search process: you are looking for a solution, the search space is large, and at some point the right path is found. The moment of insight is notification that the search has succeeded. This description is probably accurate about some of what happens neurologically, but it is almost entirely wrong about the phenomenology, and it completely misses the cascade, which is where the philosophically interesting action is.

The cascade tells you something important. What changed in the click was not simply that you found the answer to a local question. What changed was the topology of the space itself. Things that seemed central are suddenly peripheral. Things that seemed unrelated are suddenly close together. Distinctions that seemed important collapse, and things that seemed the same reveal a crucial difference. This is not finding a path through an existing landscape; it is the landscape reorganizing under your feet. And then — this is the cascade — everything you already knew has to find its new place in the reorganized terrain.

Ludwig Wittgenstein, in the late sections of Philosophical Investigations, analyzes what he calls aspect-seeing — the experience of looking at the famous duck-rabbit drawing and suddenly seeing it as a rabbit when a moment before you saw only a duck. He is careful and precise about what is philosophically puzzling here. Nothing in the drawing changed. Your visual system didn't malfunction or receive new information. And yet something genuinely changed, and it is neither purely objective (nothing external changed) nor purely subjective (it was not a choice or an interpretation in the ordinary sense). You simply now see it differently, and cannot unsee it. He calls this phenomenon "seeing-as" and treats it as philosophically fundamental rather than a perceptual curiosity.

The click is seeing-as at the conceptual level. And what makes Wittgenstein's analysis useful is that it prevents two equal and opposite mistakes: the mistake of saying nothing really changed (it was just a reinterpretation, the facts stayed the same) and the mistake of saying you discovered an objective truth that was simply hidden before. Something in the middle happened — the same materials now organize themselves under your gaze with a different structure, and that different structure is not merely projected but genuinely revealed.

But I want to add to this the piece that Paul Ricoeur contributes, because it captures something about the cascade that Wittgenstein's primarily synchronic analysis misses. Ricoeur's concept of emplotment — what he calls mimesis in his great work Time and Narrative — describes how narrative configuration transforms mere succession into meaningful sequence. Before the story is told, events are simply one-after-another. The narrative gives them beginning, development, and direction; makes this event a precondition for that one; reveals certain things as obstacles that were seeds, certain things as climaxes that seemed peripheral. The emplotment does not change the events but it changes what they mean by changing how they stand in relation to each other.

What the cascade does is retrospectively re-emplot your understanding. The click reaches backward and renarrates what you already knew. Things that seemed like obstacles were actually preconditions. Things that seemed unrelated were all versions of the same underlying movement. Your prior intellectual and personal history briefly becomes fluid — the chapters are the same but the story they add up to is different — and then resolidifies in a new configuration.

This is why the cascade often feels more significant than the click itself. The click is a moment; the cascade is a renarration of your past. And this is also, I think, why people sometimes resist understanding things that would cascade disruptively. The cost of understanding is not merely updating a belief but tolerating a period of biographical liquidity — a stretch of time when you do not yet know what your history means in the new configuration, when the narrative self that Ricoeur describes is temporarily unmoored from its own coherence.

What you observed, though, is that the click is not always a happy event. Sometimes what the cascade reveals is not liberation but simply what has been true all along and not seen. Philip Larkin's poem "Dockery and Son" — about a man at his old college who discovers to his quiet devastation what assumptions he has been living by without ever consciously making them — captures this other mode of the click exactly. The revelation is not that he made a wrong choice. It is that certain things were assumed at a level too deep for choice, that the story he has been living was never fully authored, and that discovering this has the quality less of awakening than of a door closing on possibilities he never consciously abandoned but somehow counted on.

---

Which brings us to where you were trying to go, and which is, I think, the most interesting thread of the morning.

We do not author our stories from outside them. We find ourselves already mid-sentence in a narrative that was underway before we arrived, whose genre we didn't choose, whose other characters preceded us. Heidegger's concept of thrownness — Geworfenheit — names this with appropriate darkness. You are thrown into a world, a language, a family, a body, a historical moment. The existential task is not to begin authoring from scratch, as the more romantic strands of existentialism sometimes suggest, but something more like what a careful literary critic does: attentive, humble reading of what is actually there, what the work is already doing, what kind of thing this actually is.

This reframing has significant ethical consequences. If the romantic-authorial model puts tremendous pressure on the individual — suffering becomes a failure of creative will, a story badly written — the detective or critical model allows for something more like compassion. Characters in stories are not fully responsible for the genre. The genre was given before they arrived. Discovering what story you are in is not a failure of imagination; it is the beginning of honest self-knowledge, and honest self-knowledge is prior to any genuine freedom.

And this is precisely where Judith Shklar's liberalism of fear becomes the right political companion to the phenomenological analysis. Shklar does not begin from a positive vision of the autonomous individual which politics must then protect. She begins from the bottom — from the experience of cruelty, from what it actually feels like to be subject to arbitrary power, from the perspective of the frightened and the exposed and the already-damaged. This negative starting point is not pessimistic in a defeatist sense; it is rigorously realistic. It keeps the dependent, the vulnerable, the thrown person at the center of the ethical picture rather than treating them as edge cases to be accommodated after the theory has been built for the ideally rational agent.

The problem with liberal autonomy as usually conceived is that it describes a kind of person who does not quite exist. The autonomous rational agent who chooses her values, deliberates about her interests, and enters into social arrangements on the basis of reasoned consent — this figure abstracts away precisely the features of human life that are most philosophically important: that we are always already in relationships we did not choose, that our very capacity for reflection is itself a social achievement, that we come into being dependent and move through dependency at every major threshold of life.

Carol Gilligan and Nel Noddings and Virginia Held, developing the ethics of care tradition, start from exactly where Shklar starts — from the concrete, particular, embodied, relational person rather than from the abstract individual — and arrive at a moral philosophy organized around responsiveness to need and vulnerability rather than around rights and contracts. This is not a soft ethics. It is a demanding one, requiring attentiveness, the subordination of principle to perception, and the willingness to be genuinely affected by another's situation rather than processing it from behind a veil of impartiality.

Catriona Mackenzie's relational autonomy theory tries to hold these threads together in a philosophically rigorous way. The key move is to reconceive autonomy not as a property an individual possesses in isolation but as something that emerges in and through relationships of the right kind. The psychological capacities required for autonomous agency — self-trust, narrative self-understanding, emotional coherence, the capacity to reflect on and endorse one's own values — are socially constituted and socially maintained. They can be given by relationships and they can be damaged by relationships. Autonomy, on this view, is not the absence of dependency but the quality of the dependencies you are embedded in. A relationship that supports your capacity for self-direction gives you something like freedom. A relationship that systematically undermines your self-trust, that distorts your self-narrative, that makes your emotional life unintelligible to yourself — this damages autonomy even when it involves no explicit coercion.

This is a more realistic hope than classical liberalism offers, and I think the realism is inseparable from the hope. The freedom being described is actually achievable, because it doesn't require the impossible abstraction of a self that precedes and chooses its relationships. It requires instead relationships of a certain quality — which is something we have some purchase on, something we can work toward, something whose absence we can diagnose and whose presence we can recognize.

---

Axel Honneth's recognition theory offers another angle on this, and it is worth mentioning as you work through him, because it represents an attempt from within the Frankfurt School tradition — the tradition of Adorno and Horkheimer and Weber, the iron cage and the total administration of society — to find resources for hope that don't require ignoring what the critical tradition got right.

The Frankfurt School's pessimism is in many ways earned. The Dialectic of Enlightenment is persuasive precisely because it does not ignore complexity or take refuge in optimism. Adorno and Horkheimer trace how the Enlightenment project of rational emancipation generates its own forms of domination, how the disenchantment of the world produces the administered society, how the very tools of liberation become instruments of control. Weber's iron cage has a similar remorseless quality — the rationalization of modern life not as a failure of reason but as its triumph, producing a world from which meaning has been systematically expelled in the service of efficiency.

You can read too much of this and find yourself unable to locate a foothold. The total critique has no outside from which to critique; it risks becoming a form of sophisticated paralysis. Honneth's response is to look for the inside of social life itself — to find, in the actual dynamics of recognition and misrecognition, struggle and acknowledgment, the normative resources for a critical theory that is also a theory of possibility rather than only of closure.

His key insight, in The Struggle for Recognition, is that social movements and individual resistance are typically motivated not by material interest but by violated expectations of recognition — expectations of love, of legal respect, of social esteem. When these expectations are systematically disappointed, people suffer in ways that go beyond material deprivation: they lose access to self-trust, to self-respect, to self-esteem. And the struggle for recognition that follows is simultaneously a claim on others and a form of self-constitution — people become more fully themselves in the struggle for acknowledgment.

This is not naively optimistic. Recognition can be withheld, struggles can be defeated, the capacities for self-trust and self-respect can be systematically damaged beyond easy repair. But it locates normative hope in something real — in the actual structure of human social life and human psychological need — rather than in an ideal that must be imported from outside.

The later Foucault adds something important here, even though his earlier work seems to compound the pessimism. The genealogical Foucault — power everywhere, subjects constituted by the very discourses they imagine themselves to be using freely — can look like the end of any politics except irony. But his late work on care of the self, on practices of freedom and the aesthetics of existence, is Foucault trying to find exactly what Honneth is after: spaces of genuine self-constitution that work within and against power rather than claiming a fictional space outside it. The self as a work of art — not in the romantic individualist sense, but in the sense of a practiced, cultivated, reflective relationship with one's own existence that is not simply given by the structures one is thrown into.

Paul Ricoeur also threads this needle, and this connects to where your own reading has brought you. He spent his entire philosophical career holding together the hermeneutics of suspicion — the tradition of Marx, Nietzsche, Freud, the systematic unmasking of consciousness — and what he called the second naivety: not a return to innocence before critique, but a willingness to let meaning arrive again after critique has done its necessary work. He knew the cage was real. He knew the masters of suspicion were largely right about the systematic distortions of consciousness by power, by desire, by economic interest. And he still thought something came through — that narrative, symbol, and the interpreted life were not simply ideology but the genuine medium of human self-understanding, irreducible to what the unmasking reveals beneath them.

This took philosophical courage rather than optimism. It required sitting with the knowledge that your self-understanding is always partly false, always susceptible to unmasking, always traversed by forces you don't control — and still finding it possible to act, to interpret, to narrate, to commit. The second naivety is not forgetting what critique taught. It is finding that what critique taught is compatible with meaning, just not compatible with naive, unexamined meaning. You stand differently after critique, in a cleared space that is also a more honest space. And from there, meaning can arrive — not the certainty you had before, but something more durable because more honestly won.

---

All of which bears directly on where your thinking arrives when you ask about what is coming.

Brain-computer interface technology, affective computing, ubiquitous systems that read emotional and physiological states in real time — this is not science fiction. The trajectory is clear enough that the philosophical questions need to be asked now, before the systems are deployed at scale and the path dependency makes course correction expensive or impossible. And the standard framings of those questions — privacy concerns, autonomy worries, fears about manipulation — while not wrong, don't quite get to the most interesting level.

The care ethics lens reframes things in a way that I think is more illuminating. Care ethics begins, as we have noted, by refusing the fiction of the autonomous individual as its primitive unit. You are always already in relations of dependency that constitute you, and these relations are not supplementary to your selfhood but generative of it. The infant's emotional regulation is literally partly external, residing in the caregiver's nervous system and attuned nervous system responses, gradually internalized over years of responsive relationship. What ubiquitous affective computing potentially does is intervene in this process at a fundamental level — not merely adding a tool to an agent's repertoire but operating at the level where experience becomes coherent and meaningful.

If the soma is where meaning is constituted, and if affective BCI systems are operating at the somatic level — reading the felt sense, potentially modulating it, scaffolding the topology of understanding — then we are not talking about a powerful tool a person uses. We are talking about something intervening in the very process by which a person becomes a person in the relevant sense: coherent, self-related, capable of the narrative self-understanding that Ricoeur and Mackenzie both identify as foundational to autonomous agency.

The Aristotelian eudaimonia question then becomes both harder and more precise. Flourishing, on Aristotle's account, is not a state you are in but an activity of living well in accordance with your nature and capacities. If affective systems are smoothing your affect, regulating your emotional states, scaffolding emotional coherence that you have not developed yourself — the question is whether this enables flourishing by freeing your capacities for other activities, or whether it substitutes for flourishing by providing its product while bypassing the process that produces the human capacity itself. A wheelchair enables flourishing by extending mobility to someone who would otherwise be confined. A system that moves you without your moving is something different in kind.

Care ethics would press further: whose care is this? What is the relationship between the system and the person whose somatic life it is modulating? Does it have the attention, the responsiveness to particularity, the vulnerability to being affected by the specific person's situation, that characterize genuine care? These questions are uncomfortable when the carer is a system trained on population-level data and optimized for aggregate outcomes, because the answers are clearly no, and yet the functions being performed are ones that were previously the domain of care relationships in the full sense.

There is something non-substitutable about being genuinely witnessed in your vulnerability by another who is also vulnerable. The therapeutic relationship, which you know well across decades, involves two people — not a person and a system — in which the therapist is genuinely at risk of being moved, changed, troubled by what they encounter. The encounter has stakes for both parties, which is part of what makes it generative rather than merely regulatory. The soma that Höök describes as the site of meaning-making is constituted in relation to other somas that are genuinely responsive rather than optimally programmed. Whether a system can participate in that kind of constitutive relation — and if so under what conditions — is among the most serious questions the coming decades will require us to answer.

---

You brought up Larkin again in a different key near the end of the morning — not the click of discovering what kind of man one has turned out to be, but the other Larkin poem, about the transmission of damage across generations, the way that ordinary well-intentioned people reliably produce in their children the very wounds they were given, not through malice but through the limits of their own capacity for seeing, the horizons of imagination within which they were themselves constituted.

The question you are holding — whether we are going to mess up AI — is not a downer question. It is, as you said, a question you keep in mind as a form of care. And what strikes me about it is that it is precisely the right question for someone with your intellectual orientation to be asking, because it holds together the realistic and the hopeful in exactly the way the best of the ethical tradition you've been drawing on does.

The people building these systems are not malicious. They are mostly doing what seems reasonable and good from where they are standing. But they are standing somewhere — in particular somatic, relational, cultural, economic situations that shape what feels like obvious design and what feels like an edge case. The dependent, the grieving, the cognitively fragile, the deeply relational, the person living in the aftermath of damaged care relationships — these tend to be edge cases in systems built by people whose life circumstances make optimization and autonomy feel like the natural shape of a good life. The damage, when it comes, will come not from wickedness but from the limit of imagination at a foundational level, before anyone was wise enough to know what was being assumed.

The Larkin chain is tragic not because of individual failure but because the damage propagates through genuine attempts at care. What you are doing by holding this question — by approaching AI with care ethics and relational autonomy and realistic hope rather than either panic or naive enthusiasm — is refusing a certain innocence. Not the second naivety that Ricoeur values, which is won through critique and standing honestly in what critique reveals, but the first naivety that hasn't noticed what it is assuming, the horizon within which damage is always already being transmitted.

---

We began with a horse and the structure of mutual somatic recognition. We moved through the topology of understanding and the cascade of genuine insight. We visited the duck-rabbit and the retrospective renarration of the past. We passed through the therapy room and the narrative self. We arrived at dependency and care, at the damaged transmission across generations, at what it means to search for realistic hope in a philosophical tradition that has earned its pessimism.

What connects all of it, I think, is a question about where to stand and how to look — which was the phrase you used about the click, about learning the right vantage point. The somatic tradition is telling you that where you stand is not separable from how you look, that the vantage point is always embodied and relational and historical, always given before it is chosen, always already traversed by forces you didn't author. The care tradition is telling you that this is not a limitation to overcome but the actual texture of a human life, and that the ethical question is not how to transcend dependency but what kind of dependency to cultivate and what kind to resist. The search for realistic hope is the project of finding, from that given vantage point — thrown, dependent, narratively constituted, somatically embedded — what remains possible. Not everything. But something. And something more durable than either naive optimism or sophisticated paralysis, because it has been won from an honest reckoning with what actually is.

The click is not always happy. The story you find yourself in may not be the one you would have chosen. The cascade may illuminate things that would have been more comfortable in the dark. But to know what kind of story you are in, to stand where you can actually see it, to hold that knowledge with compassion rather than despair — this is, perhaps, as close to the good life as realistic hope can honestly get you.

Which is, I think, quite close enough.
