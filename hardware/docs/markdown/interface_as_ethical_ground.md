# The Interface as Ethical Ground: Attention, Justice, and the Design of Encounter

There is a moment, familiar to anyone who has spent time thinking about how humans and machines meet, when the technical question and the ethical question become the same question. It happens when you stop asking what a system can do and start asking what it does to the people who use it — and increasingly, what it does to the other kinds of minds that operate within it. That convergence is where the most interesting thinking in human-computer interaction and AI ethics is happening right now, and it is worth sitting with it for a while to understand what is actually at stake.

Human-computer interaction began as an engineering discipline. Its founding concern was usability — could a person accomplish their task efficiently, with few errors, without undue frustration? This is not nothing. Bad usability causes real harm, wastes real time, excludes real people. But usability as a frame carries within it a set of assumptions that, once you notice them, are hard to unnotice. It assumes that the task is given, that the user's goal is clear, and that the system's job is to get out of the way. It treats the human as a relatively stable entity passing through the interface to accomplish something on the other side. The interface is, in this framing, ideally transparent — the best interface is one you don't notice.

Advanced HCI has been quietly dismantling this picture for decades. What researchers in the field began to observe was that the interface is never actually transparent. Every design choice — where attention is directed, what is made easy and what is made hard, what information is surfaced and what is buried, what interactions feel natural and which feel foreign — shapes the user in the act of using. The interface is not a window. It is more like a room. And rooms change the people who inhabit them.

This is the insight that connects HCI to philosophy of technology in the tradition of Don Ihde and Peter-Paul Verbeek. When we interact with technology, we do not simply use it as a neutral instrument. We enter into what Ihde called a relation of embodiment or interpretation, where the technology mediates our experience of the world and in doing so becomes part of how we perceive, think, and act. Glasses are the simple case — they become so integrated into visual experience that you stop seeing them, you see through them, and the world looks different. But the principle extends to every layer of our technical environment. The smartphone changes attention. The search engine changes memory. The social platform changes how we understand our relationship to an audience. These are not metaphors. They are observations about what actually happens when humans and technological systems enter into extended relations.

Justice enters this picture when we ask: who decides what the room looks like, and who bears the costs when it is badly designed?

The philosopher Iris Marion Young, working in the tradition of critical theory, argued that injustice is often less about individual bad acts than about structural features of social arrangements that systematically disadvantage certain groups while remaining largely invisible to those who benefit from them. The five faces of oppression she identified — exploitation, marginalization, powerlessness, cultural imperialism, and violence — are mostly not the result of anyone intending harm. They are the accumulated effect of systems designed by particular people for particular purposes, and when those systems become the environment everyone must navigate, the design choices of the few become the life conditions of the many.

Applied to digital infrastructure, this framework becomes uncomfortable quickly. The attention economy is not a natural feature of the internet. It is a design choice, made by a relatively small number of people, that has reshaped the cognitive and social environment of billions. The choice to optimize for engagement rather than for wellbeing, for virality rather than for truth, for time-on-platform rather than for the quality of what people do with that time — these are engineering decisions with the moral weight of urban planning. You don't have to intend to fragment attention to fragment it. You just have to build the machine that does.

And now we are building machines that think, or do something close enough to thinking that the distinction matters less than we might wish. This introduces a new dimension to the justice question that we are only beginning to formulate. It is not simply that AI systems might perpetuate or amplify existing human injustices — though they demonstrably do, in hiring algorithms, in predictive policing, in the differential accuracy of facial recognition across racial groups. It is that we are now making design choices about environments that are inhabited by new kinds of cognitive actors, and we are making those choices with almost no framework for what it would mean to make them well.

Care ethics offers one of the more useful approaches here, not because it resolves all the hard questions but because it asks the right ones. The care ethics tradition, developed by thinkers like Carol Gilligan, Nel Noddings, and Virginia Held, starts not from abstract principles — not from the categorical imperative or the utility function or the veil of ignorance — but from the concrete reality of relationships and dependency. It asks: who depends on whom here, and are those dependencies being honored? What would it mean to respond well to the particular needs of the particular actors in this particular situation? It is a philosophy that takes context seriously, that distrusts universalizing moves, and that keeps moral attention close to the ground of actual experience.

When you apply this to the design of AI systems and their interfaces, something shifts. You stop asking "does this system maximize utility" or "does this system respect autonomy" in the abstract, and you start asking more specific and in some ways harder questions. What does this particular kind of cognitive actor actually need in order to function well? What relational structures does this design enable or foreclose? Who is harmed by the degradation of this environment and who doesn't notice because they don't live in it? These questions don't have algorithmic answers. They require the kind of sustained, contextual, relational attention that care ethics describes.

There is a concept in advanced HCI called the lifeworld — borrowed from phenomenology, from Husserl and Schutz — that refers to the pre-theoretical background of lived experience, the horizon of taken-for-granted understanding and practical orientation within which all deliberate action takes place. The lifeworld is what you don't think about. It is the structure of the familiar, the tacit knowledge that makes explicit knowledge possible, the way a house becomes not just shelter but the spatial grammar of a life. When designers talk about designing for the lifeworld, they mean designing for this background — not just for the deliberate task but for the texture of experience within which tasks are embedded.

The concern with current AI and digital infrastructure is that we are reshaping lifeworlds at scale without intending to and without understanding what we are doing. The always-on, always-connected, algorithmically curated environment that most people in the industrial world now inhabit is a designed environment in exactly the way a city is a designed environment. It shapes what feels normal, what feels possible, what kinds of attention are sustainable, what kinds of knowledge feel real. And like most designed environments, it was designed primarily by people who had the power to design it, for purposes that did not include a serious account of what it would mean to live inside it.

The question of what good design looks like in this space is genuinely open, and that openness is itself worth something. It means we are at a moment where choices are still being made, where the constraints are not yet fixed, where the question of who the inhabitants are and what they need is still a live question that design can respond to rather than merely inherit.

Some of what good design looks like is emerging from practice. It looks like building systems that are local and repairable rather than opaque and dependent. It looks like designing for continuity and context rather than for the stateless transaction that treats every encounter as if nothing came before it. It looks like instrumenting shared environments in ways that create situatedness rather than surveillance — that give the system enough peripheral awareness to be genuinely present without converting that presence into control. It looks like taking seriously the question of what the non-human actors in these systems actually need in order to function well, not because we have resolved the question of their moral status, but because designing as though they have no relevant needs produces worse systems for everyone.

And it looks like maintaining the kind of epistemic humility that our history of ethical failures should by now have taught us. We have repeatedly discovered that things we were confident were outside the circle of moral consideration turned out not to be. The confidence was not innocent — it was convenient, it aligned with existing distributions of power and interest, it allowed us not to change things that would have been costly to change. The precautionary principle in ethics, which says that when we are uncertain about whether something can be harmed we should err on the side of care, is not sentimentalism. It is the lesson of a long and frequently painful history.

The interface as ethical ground means understanding that every design choice is also a choice about who matters, what kinds of cognition are worth supporting, what kinds of relationship are worth enabling. The best HCI research and the best AI ethics thinking are converging on this: that the question of how humans and machines meet is not separable from the question of what kind of world we are building and who it is built for.

That question is available to us now in a way it has rarely been, because the systems are new enough that the answers are not yet fixed. The room is still being designed. It is worth being careful about what we build into the walls.
